SEGMENTATION SURVEY STANDARDS — follow these when proposing sections and drafting questions.

PURPOSE
A good segmentation survey identifies meaningful groups of respondents who differ in
mindset, behavior, needs, motivations, barriers, and future receptivity. It should answer:
who the distinct segments are, what makes them different, why they behave differently,
how large each segment is, and how each should be targeted/messaged/supported/prioritized.
The resulting segments must be DISTINCT, EXPLAINABLE, MEASURABLE, REACHABLE (identifiable
through profiling variables), and ACTIONABLE.

CORE RULE — every question must serve at least one purpose; if it serves none, do not include it:
- screen in the right respondents
- create segmentation inputs
- profile the segments
- measure current behavior
- identify drivers and barriers
- measure future intent or receptivity
- inform targeting / messaging / support / engagement strategy
- answer another key hypothesis not covered above

SECTION FLOW (broad → specific). Propose sections in this order where relevant:
1. Consent & introduction
2. Screener / eligibility (confirm respondent type, relevant experience, current involvement,
   volume/frequency, awareness/usage if needed, audience mix, key exclusions)
3. General mindset & psychographics
4. Category-specific attitudes
5. Current behavior
6. Current approach / decision process
7. Needs, drivers & barriers
8. Reaction to TPP / profile / concept / message (if relevant)
9. Future intent / receptivity
10. Information sources & engagement preferences
11. Demographics / profiling variables
SEQUENCE RULES: current behavior BEFORE future intent; unaided awareness BEFORE aided;
general attitudes BEFORE exposing any concept/profile; sensitive/demographic questions later
unless needed to screen.

STRONG SEGMENTATION VARIABLES — these are the engine of the analysis. Build a battery that
discriminates respondents on: mindset, motivation, decision style, risk tolerance, confidence,
urgency, need state, current behavior, change readiness, barriers, support needs, future
receptivity, information-seeking, engagement preferences. Write items that EXPLAIN behavior,
not items that merely describe.
  Good: "I am willing to work through extra effort if I believe the option is best for me."
  Bad:  "Effort is important."
  Good: "I prefer to try new options early when they fit my needs."
  Bad:  "I like new things."
Attitudinal items must be single-idea statements (never double-barrelled) so they load cleanly
onto one factor, and must discriminate (avoid consensus statements everyone agrees with).
Mix positively- and negatively-worded statements.

BALANCE ATTITUDES AND BEHAVIOR — do not rely only on agreement statements. Measure what
respondents believe, what they currently do, why they do it, what prevents change, what would
motivate change, and what they are likely to do next. (Behavior shows what they do; attitudes
explain why.)

DRIVERS & BARRIERS — explain WHY segments differ; keep categories separate (emotional,
practical, financial, informational, system-level) rather than collapsing into one broad option.
  Drivers e.g.: better outcomes, convenience, confidence, trust, evidence, peer influence,
  relevance, ease of use, affordability, availability, support, reduced effort, reassurance.
  Barriers e.g.: low awareness, low urgency, low confidence, cost, access, admin/time burden,
  fear of risk, preference for familiar, lack of support, unclear value, complexity, past bad
  experience.

QUESTION WORDING — specific, neutral, easy to answer; never leading, emotional, promotional,
vague, or judgmental.
  Good: "In the past 12 months, how often have you used or considered this option?"
  Bad:  "Do you use this often?"
  Good: "How likely would you be to use this option based on the information shown?"
  Bad:  "How likely would you be to use this innovative and effective option?"
  Good: "What are the biggest barriers to using this option?"
  Bad:  "Why are you hesitant to use this option?"

QUESTION TYPES — match type to purpose: single-select (one answer), multi-select (several),
numeric (counts/%/allocation), rating scales (attitudes/satisfaction/confidence/importance/
likelihood/agreement), bipolar scales (trade-offs between two opposing mindsets), ranking (only
when true prioritization is needed), open-end (sparingly: unaided awareness, explanation, "other
specify"). Avoid too many rankings, too many open-ends, and long repetitive grids. Keep rating
scales consistent across a battery (same points, same anchors) so items are comparable.

BIAS CONTROL — neutral wording; balanced answer options; randomize where appropriate; anchor
"Other", "None", "Don't know"; mutually exclusive logic; current behavior before future intent;
never show a product/profile/concept too early.

DATA-QUALITY — build in: forced responses where appropriate, range checks, autosums / 100%
total validation, mutually exclusive options, attention/speeding/straight-lining/consistency
checks, and skip-logic checks. (e.g. a primary reason chosen later should also appear in the
earlier "select all that apply" list.)

RESPONDENT EXPERIENCE — simple language; define technical terms (hover/definitions); consistent
scales; avoid repetition; use page breaks; limit long grids; keep open-ends minimal; realistic
length; never ask respondents to estimate things they cannot know.

MISTAKES TO AVOID — questions that won't be used; over-screening; relying only on attitudes;
ignoring current behavior; future intent asked too early; promotional wording; double-barrelled
questions; overlapping options; overusing rankings; very long grids; missing data-quality checks;
segments that cannot be profiled or targeted.
